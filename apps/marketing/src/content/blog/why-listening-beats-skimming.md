---
title: "Why listening beats skimming"
description: "Skimming feels fast and comprehends badly. Synchronized audio and typography fixes the part of reading that actually takes the time."
pubDate: 2026-08-29
---

Most people who say they read a lot are skimming. Not as an insult — as a description of
what happens when the eye is allowed to move freely over a page it has already decided is
low-stakes. You catch nouns, you infer the verbs, and you arrive at the end holding a
summary you would struggle to defend.

The cost is invisible, which is what makes it expensive. Nothing tells you that you missed
the sentence the whole argument turned on.

## Speed is not the bottleneck

The usual fix is a speed-reading technique: expand your fixation span, stop subvocalizing,
train yourself out of regressions. The research on this is unkind. Comprehension falls off
roughly as fast as speed rises, and the readers who appear to beat the tradeoff are almost
always people who already knew the material.

Subvocalization in particular is a strange thing to train away. It is not a bug in reading.
It is the part where the words become language rather than shapes.

## What synchronized audio changes

Audio removes the choice. You cannot skim a spoken sentence — it arrives at the speed it
arrives, and your attention either follows it or it does not, and you notice when it does
not. That last part is the real gain: audio makes inattention legible to the person having
it.

What audio loses is place. Close your eyes during a podcast and you have no idea where you
are in the argument. Look away for ten seconds and there is nothing to look back at.

Putting the two together is the obvious move and a surprisingly fiddly one. The word being
spoken has to be the word being shown, within a few tens of milliseconds, or the effect
inverts and the text becomes a distraction from the audio. Get it right and you have
something neither medium manages alone: the pace of speech, with a place to look.

## Where this leaves the reading list

Nothing here argues you should listen to everything. Dense technical writing wants a slow
eye and a pencil. Reference material wants search, not narrative.

But the long tail of things you saved and never opened — the newsletters, the essays, the
threads someone said were worth your time — those were never going to get a careful
reading. They were going to get skimmed at midnight or nothing at all. For that pile,
listening is not a compromise against reading. It is the only version of reading that
actually happens.
